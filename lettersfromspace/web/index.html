Letters from Space

A hands-on lab about how a neural network learns. Professor Manso's camera filmed 20,000 frames of night sky, and a message is hidden in them, one letter at a time.

Train a small model for each letter (a neural network, trained with back-propagation) and watch what it learns, from the examples it is shown to the weights of each hidden unit. Then let each model search the whole video, check what it finds, and read the message. Along the way: what makes good training data, why being right on the examples is not enough, and how sure a model must be before you believe it.

The story from the 2001 practical at ESI, Bobo-Dioulasso

Inspired by the posters around ESI, Professor Manso became fascinated by the possibility of life beyond Earth. One night, driving back from Nasso, he saw a group of lights in the sky that looked a little like the letter A.

He is far too busy preparing his lectures to spend his nights watching the stars, so he pointed a video camera at that part of the sky. Unfortunately, watching the video takes as long as watching the sky: one night holds thousands of frames, far too many to inspect by hand.

Using what you know about multi-layer neural networks, you can build a network that runs through every frame of the video looking for one particular letter. Working together, with one network per letter, the class can put the letters back in order and, Professor Manso hopes, receive some wisdom from outer space.

Training data

A detector learns from examples: positives that are the letter, and negatives that are not. Pick a letter, look at the examples the model will learn from, and change how they are made.

The video's letters have 1 to 4 flipped pixels, so the positives get noisy copies of the letter's samples (every letter gets the same number of copies). About 98% of the video is empty sky with random stars; the empty-sky negatives show the model what "nothing" looks like: the 2001 class trained with letters only. With new noise every epoch, the noisy copies and the sky are drawn again before every pass, so the model cannot memorise a few examples; the frames below are those of the first epoch.

The model and back-propagation

    
One model per letter, a detector that answers a single question: is this frame the letter? Each model is a
      small neural network with 64 inputs (one per pixel), a hidden layer, and a single output unit that should say 1.0 for the
      letter and 0.0 for anything else. Back-propagation shows it every example, measures the error and nudges every
      weight to make the error smaller, epoch after epoch.


    

      

        

          
          
          
          
          
          
        

        

          
          
        

        
The 2001 settings: 16 hidden units, learning rate 0.2, momentum 0.9, stop when the epoch error (the sum over all
          examples of ½·(target − output)²) falls below 0.01. Weights start as small random numbers between 0 and 0.2. A run that
          gets stuck without reaching that error starts again from new random weights (up to 3 runs).

Message from space

    
In 2001 each pair of students hunted two letters and the class merged its lists of frame numbers. Here the
      computer does the merging: every detection is sorted by frame number, and when two detectors claim the same frame the higher
      score wins. Train and scan every letter at once with the settings above, or one letter at a time in steps 2 and 3.

    Letters from Space is a neural-network practical lab at ESI, Université Polytechnique de Bobo-Dioulasso, from 2001: Professor
    Manso's story about Letters from Space, the video with its hidden poem, and the network his students trained. This English
    edition (2026) still runs that network, the same hand-written Java code the students used, one object per neuron and per
    synapse. prepared by David Croft, Burkina, 2001
    

    
On this page the network runs in your own browser, one training thread per processor core:
      a line-by-line JavaScript translation of that Java code, with the same objects, the same arithmetic and the same random
      numbers, so a seed trains exactly as it does in Java. Nothing is computed on a server.
